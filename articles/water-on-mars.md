# Water on Mars: What We Know and Why It Matters

**Last updated:** May 20, 2026

For centuries, humans have speculated about whether our planetary neighbor Mars once held — or still holds — liquid water. Early astronomers observed white polar caps and seasonal dark patches, interpreting them as oceans and vegetation. Today, a fleet of orbiters, landers, and rovers has transformed speculation into science. The answer, it turns out, is both subtle and profound: water exists on Mars, but not in the way early observers imagined. It is locked in polar ice, buried as subsurface brine, frozen in ancient permafrost, and intermittently present as vapor in the thin atmosphere. This article explores how we know, what the evidence shows, and what it means.

## How We Detect Water on Mars

Detecting water on a planet tens of millions of kilometers away requires ingenuity. Scientists use three principal methods: radar sounding, spectroscopy, and geomorphological analysis.

### Radar Sounding: Seeing Beneath the Surface

Ground-penetrating radar works much like medical ultrasound, but instead of sound waves, it uses radio waves to image subsurface structures. Two instruments have been critical: **MARSIS** (Mars Advanced Radar for Subsurface and Ionosphere Sounding), carried by the European Space Agency's Mars Express orbiter, and **SHARAD** (Shallow Radar) on NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.

These instruments send radio pulses toward the planet and measure the echoes that bounce back from different layers. When radio waves pass through material with high **dielectric permittivity** — a measure of how easily a material transmits an electric field — it signals the presence of liquid water. In 2018, scientists using MARSIS data announced the detection of a bright subsurface reflector beneath the Martian south pole, interpreted as a 20-kilometer-wide reservoir of liquid brine [^1]. The water stays liquid despite temperatures well below freezing because it is supersaturated with magnesium and calcium perchlorate salts — the same compounds used in some commercial de-icers on Earth. The discovery was later reinforced by additional data suggesting the lake may be part of a wider network of subsurface brine bodies [^2].

### Spectroscopy: Reading Light

Every material absorbs and reflects light at characteristic wavelengths. **Spectroscopy** is the technique of splitting light into its component wavelengths (like a rainbow) and identifying which wavelengths are missing — the "spectral signature" of specific molecules. Different instruments on orbiters like Mars Express, Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, and the now-retired Mars Global Surveyor carry spectrometers tuned to different parts of the electromagnetic spectrum.

The **OMEGA** spectrometer on Mars Express mapped hydrated minerals across the planet's surface, identifying clays (phyllosilicates) formed in the presence of liquid water billions of years ago [^3]. Meanwhile, the **CRISM** spectrometer on the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter produced high-resolution mineral maps showing opaline silica and iron-rich clays — minerals that form in water-rich environments. In the atmosphere, spectrometers have detected water vapor at concentrations of roughly 100 parts per million, varying seasonally as water sublimates from the polar caps.

### Geomorphology: Reading the Land

Before we could probe beneath the surface or analyze minerals from orbit, telescopes revealed features that looked unmistakably like water-carved landscapes. **Valley networks** — branching systems of channels that resemble river drainages on Earth — snake across the ancient southern highlands. **Deltas** at the mouths of these channels, most famously at Jezero Crater (the landing site of NASA's Perseverance rover), show the classic fan-shaped deposits formed when flowing water meets a standing body [^4].

These features tell us that Mars was not always the cold, arid world it is today. During the Noachian period (roughly 4.1 to 3.7 billion years ago), the planet had a thicker atmosphere and a hydrological cycle vigorous enough to carve these landscapes. The presence of lakebeds — some with layered sediments that would have taken tens of thousands of years to accumulate — points to long-standing water bodies, not just brief floods [^5].

## The Evidence: Where the Water Is

Today, water on Mars resides in four main reservoirs:

### Polar Ice Caps

Both the north and south polar caps contain water ice. The northern cap is predominantly water ice, while the southern cap is largely carbon dioxide ice (dry ice) overlying a water ice base. Combined, the polar caps hold enough water that if melted, it would cover the entire planet in a layer roughly 20 meters deep [^6].

### Subsurface Brine Lakes

The MARSIS-detected subglacial lake beneath the south pole — named **Ultimi Scopuli** after the surrounding terrain — remains the most dramatic recent discovery. Liquid water requires both sufficient pressure and a freezing-point depressant. The overlying 1.5 kilometers of ice provides the pressure, while perchlorate salts provide the antifreeze. Subsequent analysis has identified additional bright radar reflections in the same region, suggesting a subglacial hydrological system [^2].

### Recurring Slope Lineae

**Recurring Slope Lineae** (RSL) are narrow, dark streaks that appear on steep slopes during warm seasons, grow over weeks, and fade when temperatures drop. First definitively identified by the High Resolution Imaging Science Experiment (HiRISE) camera, RSL were initially hailed as strong evidence for contemporary liquid water flowing on the surface [^7]. However, later analysis has complicated this picture. While the seasonal behavior strongly suggests a role for water — possibly deliquescent salts absorbing atmospheric moisture — some studies argue that dry granular flows can explain the features without invoking liquid water at all. The debate remains active.

### Atmospheric Vapor and Permafrost

Mars's atmosphere contains trace amounts of water vapor, saturating at about 100% humidity near the surface during northern summer. This water cycles between the atmosphere and surface frost on a daily and seasonal basis. Deep beneath the surface, vast regions of permafrost — permanently frozen ground — extend for kilometers, representing the largest water reservoir by volume.

## What It Means: Habitability and the Search for Life

Where there is liquid water on Earth, there is life — in deep subsurface aquifers, in Antarctic lakes buried under kilometers of ice, in ultra-saline pools, and in the perpetually dark deep ocean. This universal association is why water detection on Mars is more than a geological curiosity.

### Past Habitability

The evidence for ancient water — valley networks, deltas, lake sediments, hydrated clay minerals — paints a picture of early Mars as a far more Earth-like world. During the Noachian, Mars likely had a hydrological cycle, rain, rivers, and lakes. NASA's Curiosity rover has identified organic molecules in 3.5-billion-year-old mudstones at Gale Crater, preserved in an ancient lakebed environment [^8]. While not evidence of life itself, these findings confirm that the raw chemical ingredients existed in a habitable environment.

### Present Habitability

The subsurface brine lakes are the most exciting target for contemporary astrobiology. If liquid water exists deep beneath the south pole, it represents an isolated, dark, cold, and saline environment — analogous to subglacial lakes in Antarctica like Lake Vostok or Lake Whillans. On Earth, these environments teem with microbial life adapted to extreme conditions. The Martian brines would be even more extreme — colder, saltier, and exposed to far higher radiation — but the principle that liquid water can support life in apparently hostile conditions is well established.

### Implications for Human Exploration

The presence of water ice directly informs plans for human exploration. Water is the heaviest commodity a crew would need to bring; if it can be extracted in situ — melted from permafrost or mined from polar ice — the mass savings are enormous. Water can be used for drinking, for growing food, for radiation shielding, and for splitting into hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel. NASA's Mars architecture explicitly relies on the ability to extract water from the Martian environment [^9].

## Open Questions

The discoveries of the past decade have answered old questions while raising new ones:

- **How extensive are the subsurface brines?** Are they isolated pockets or part of a regional groundwater system? We need more radar surveys and, ideally, a dedicated mission to probe the south polar region.
- **Do recurring slope lineae involve liquid water?** The debate between wet and dry hypotheses will likely require in-situ measurements that current rovers cannot make — the features are on steep slopes that are inaccessible to wheeled vehicles.
- **Is there any extant life?** This is the ultimate question. No current or planned mission can directly test for life in the subsurface brine lakes. Drilling kilometers through ice on another planet remains a distant capability.
- **How much water is actually accessible?** For human missions, we need to know not just that water exists, but how easily it can be extracted — its purity, depth, and form (ice, hydrated minerals, adsorbed moisture).

## Future Missions

Several missions aim to close these gaps. **ESA's ExoMars Rosalind Franklin rover** (delayed from previous launch windows) carries a drill capable of reaching 2 meters depth, where organic molecules may be preserved. NASA's **Mars Sample Return** campaign — a multi-mission effort to bring samples collected by Perseverance back to Earth — will allow laboratory analysis of materials from Jezero Crater's ancient delta. Concepts for a **Mars Ice Mapper** orbiter would specifically target shallow water ice deposits at higher resolution than current instruments. And in the longer term, the **Mars Life Explorer** mission concept, recommended by the National Academies' Planetary Science Decadal Survey, would land in a polar region to access and analyze subsurface ice for signs of life.
